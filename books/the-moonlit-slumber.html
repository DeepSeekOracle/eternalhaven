Book I · Volume I of the Silver Accord

The Moonlit Slumber

Night, a vow, and the first lantern. The book that starts the saga slowly on purpose.

JH
Justin Helmer
A site introduction · updated 6 November 2026 · about a nine-minute read

Every saga has a door that looks like a wall until you are through it. The Moonlit Slumber is that door. It is not the loudest book in the series, and it is not meant to be. It is the one where a promise is made in front of witnesses who are still deciding whether they mean it.

If you have been handed this book by someone who said "start here," they were right, and they were also asking you for patience. The first hundred pages are a place, not a plot. Elysun Vale, the high eaves, pine and lark and watchfire. The reader who wants a battle by chapter four will be bored by a book that is busy teaching them what a vow costs.

Where it begins

The prologue is called The Lullaby of Skylark, and it does the thing I care most about in fantasy: it establishes a cosmology without turning it into a lecture. There was a dawn before the first dawn. In the quiet of that hour a song was woven, and the song became the First Seal. The old formula for it appears in the text as an equation — the integral of light and truth over the world — and the people of the Vale had a plainer name for it: the homeward sound.

The Seal chose a vessel. In those days it chose a young rider named Arin, whose laughter outran fear, and who grew up where the wind drinks the scent of pine and lark. That is the novel's first promise: the sacred is not abstract here, it is carried by someone with mud on his boots.

What happens, without giving it away

Fifteen chapters, and their titles are honest about the shape of the story. Serenya and the Bronze Emberion. The Whispering Moon. The Call of Lyra. Illusions at Dusk. Corvath, the Fallen Dragon. Then the middle of the book, where a community does its best and its worst: Bonds in Trial, The Festival of Accord, Serenya's Temptation, The Haven's Secrets, Betrayal at Moonrise.

The last movement is where the saga earns its name. Emberion's Trial tests the one character who has the most power and the least practice at restraint. The Shattering of Accord is the chapter that every later book is answering. The Last Song of Lyra is the one readers write to me about. Emberion Ascends sounds like triumph and is not simple. The final chapter, The Moonlit Slumber, gives the whole volume its title, and the epilogue asks a question with a question mark in it: A New Accord?

What I will tell you is that no one in this book is a villain in their own head. Corvath is the closest thing to a monster, and the chapter that introduces him is careful with the word fallen — fallen from what, and by whose push.

What the book is arguing

Three arguments run under the plot. The first is that unity is a practice, not a mood. The Accord is not a vibe; it is sentences that people agreed to remember, and the book is interested in what happens when remembering becomes optional. The second is that moonlight is a clock. In the Haven, night is not decoration. It tells you who kept a watch and who sold the watch for a speech. The third is that power under constraint is the only interesting kind. Emberion begins in bronze and ends the book changed; the question the novel keeps asking him is the one I care about in every system I build: what do you do with capability after you have seen what it can break?

"The elders say there was a dawn before the first dawn; a quiet hour when the world held its breath and listened."

That is the first line of the prologue. It is also the thesis. A saga about listening is a strange thing to write in an era of clips, which is probably why I kept writing it.

How to read it

Slowly, and out loud if you can. The prose was built to be spoken. Names accumulate rather than dump on page one, so you do not need a glossary to start; you need to trust that the book will tell you who matters. If you are audio-first, the narrated edition exists and is listed from the hub. If you are reading on a phone at midnight, one chapter at a time is the intended pace.

Do not skip to Book III because war sounds more exciting. The war is only expensive because of what was agreed in this book. A reader who starts at the siege has arrived at a funeral without knowing the person.

Where the copy lives

Two official doors, both mine, neither an affiliate link. Paper: The Moonlit Slumber, paperback on Lulu. Audio: Book I, narrated, on YouTube. The narration is mine, not a licensed reader's, so the voices are the ones I heard while writing.

I do not keep six storefronts on this domain pretending to be a shop. If a door is closed when you try it, write to me and I will point you at the copy that is actually in print rather than the one a template says should exist. The other volumes are gathered on the shelf.
