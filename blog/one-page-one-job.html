One page, one job

The rule that fixed two sites: every page answers exactly one question, in prose, before it asks for anything. It is not a growth tactic. It is a test.

JH
Justin Helmer
6 November 2026 · about a ten-minute read

A page can fail in a way that no screenshot reveals. It can load in half a second, look expensive, and still be a hole in the ground: nothing on it answers the question that brought the visitor. When an advertising review calls that "low value content," it is using crude language for a real observation. The page was a doorway with no room behind it.

I have now rebuilt two domains under one rule, and the rule is small enough to write on an index card. Every page answers exactly one question, in prose, before it asks for anything.

The rule in practice

Pick the question. For a book page it is "should I read this, and what is it?" For a tools page it is "which of these is worth my evening?" For a contact page it is "can I reach a human?" If you cannot say the question in one sentence, the page is two pages wearing one URL, and one of them is going to be thin.

Then answer it in paragraphs. Not bullets, not cards, not an accordion. Paragraphs, because a paragraph is where a claim has to survive contact with a reader. A card that says "AI-powered publishing workflow" can be produced by anyone in four seconds; a sentence that explains what happens when you press publish cannot.

Then, and only then, ask. Sign up, buy, play, contact. The order matters more than the design. A request made before the answer is a toll booth, and people correctly refuse to pay tolls for nothing.

What it fixed

The first domain I did this on had a music catalog. Every page was a shell with buttons: listen here, buy there, follow this. The prose lived nowhere. Rebuilding it meant writing introductions that only the artist could have written — what a track cost to make, why a licence is worded the way it is, which door is a backup and which is the front. The site got slower to build and much faster to trust.

The second domain is this one. It had essays already, and essays are not the problem. The problem was the scaffolding around them: ten pages that were only a redirect, a book section that listed five titles with no introduction, a contact page with an address and nothing else. The essays were a library; the front of the house was a corridor of doors that opened onto other buildings.

The three failures the rule catches

The stub. A page whose whole content is a link to somewhere else. It exists so a URL does not 404, which is a legitimate technical need and an illegitimate reading experience. If the thing moved, say what it was and why it moved, then point at the door. That is a page. A meta-refresh is a shrug.

The directory. A page that is a list of other pages. Useful once — a shelf, an index — and then it multiplies. A domain of directories is a maze with no rooms. Even a listing page should tell you how to choose, which is a sentence a directory cannot say.

The duplicate. The same paragraph on four pages because it was easier than writing four paragraphs. Readers notice before crawlers do, and they notice as boredom. If a paragraph applies to everything, put it on one page and link to it from the others.

What it costs

Time, mostly, and the unpleasant experience of discovering that a page you were proud of has no job. Half the work of the rule is subtraction: closing a page into a redirect, or folding it into a better page, or admitting that a section was a mood rather than a subject.

It also costs the comfort of numbers. A directory page with forty links looks productive in a dashboard. A page that answers one question in nine hundred words looks like less until a human reads it. I have chosen the second kind of looking, because the first kind is how you end up with a site that a reviewer can describe in one word.

How to test a page in sixty seconds

Read the page out loud and stop after the first two paragraphs. Can you say what question it answers? If not, it is a stub. Could a different site have published those two paragraphs unchanged? If yes, it is filler. Does the page ask for something before it gives something? If yes, the order is wrong.

Then look at the link you would click next. If the page's only real content is that link, the page is a hallway and the room is elsewhere. Hallways are allowed in a house. A house that is only hallways is a maze, and mazes are what reviewers mean when they say a site has no value.

The rule is not a formula for winning a review. It is a formula for not wasting a reader's evening. Those two things overlap more than the industry admits. The start page is the test applied to this house: one page, one job, which is to send you to exactly one other page.
