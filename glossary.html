The Haven glossary

Every name the books and the workshop share, defined once, in plain language. Look up one word and leave.

JH
Justin Helmer
Reference page · updated 6 November 2026 · about a twelve-minute read

Two vocabularies meet on this domain and they share words on purpose. The saga uses seal as a metaphysical guarantee; the workshop uses it as a hash, a signature, a receipt. This page defines both, marks which is which, and does not ask you to care about the other one.

The books

Accord. A sentence people agreed to remember. The saga treats accords as characters rather than documents: they are born, strained, shattered, and re-forged, and the interesting question is never whether the text survived but whether the spirit did.

The Silver Accord. The name of the first volume's arc and the old order it describes. Silver because it was made early and it tarnishes.

The Shattered Accord. Book II, and also the event: the night the old agreement stops holding. The phrase is used for both because in these books the event and the book are the same object.

The First Seal. The seal woven before the first dawn, when the world held its breath and listened. It chose Arin as its vessel. Its old formula is an integral of light and truth over the world, which the people of the Vale called the homeward sound.

Seal (fiction). A localised guarantee. A seal says this holds, and can be produced later to prove it. Book III introduces seals forged from lived suffering rather than granted from above; the saga calls them alive, which means they can fail, which is the point.

Seal chain (fiction). What you get when seals depend on each other. Break one and you invalidate every promise leaning on it. This is the saga's core political mechanism and, not by coincidence, how software dependencies work.

Aurethys. The world under the Haven. Seen from above in Book II, abandoned in Book III, inhabited again in Book IV.

Elysun Vale. The high country where Arin grows up: pine, lark, watchfires, cliff ledges. It falls in Book II, and the saga loses its ability to pretend the war is elsewhere.

The Circle of Haven. A crown of crystalline spires above Aurethys, with a ceiling of living light that moves with the world's heartbeat. Where the Council meets and where the current first falters.

The Broken Moon. Corvath's seat after the fracture. A structure that can be occupied, not just a romantic object. Gone by Book IV, replaced by rivers of silver light.

The Choir. Many voices. A single voice can be wrong; a choir can be corrupted. Harmony is what you get when disagreement is still legible, not when everyone agrees.

Imperfect light. The saga's name for the honest version of illumination: not omniscience, orientation. Enough light to walk without lying about what you see. Book IV's closing accord is named for it.

The Council of Twelve. The decision body. Eleven until Corvath is seated in Book IV. It begins as an ideal and becomes a room where reasonable people produce casualties.

Homeward sound. The Vale's plain name for the First Seal. The kind of name a place gives a thing it has to live with.

The workshop

LYGO. The name I use for the workshop's public systems: small, local-first tools with published receipts. It is not a company and not a religion. If a page uses the word and you do not need the tools, you can skip the page.

Δ9 (Delta Nine). The shorthand for the protocol family and its council of personas. It appears in the books' front matter because the fiction and the systems grew in the same room, not because the novels are documentation.

Seal (workshop). A hash or signed record that proves a file has not changed. Same idea as the fiction, different materials: where the book's seal is a vow, the workshop's seal is a SHA-256.

Hash. A fixed-length fingerprint of a file. Change one byte and the fingerprint changes. Cheap to compute, impossible to reverse. This is the whole technology behind the ebook hash.

Merkle root. One hash that stands for a whole set of files. Change any file in the set and the root changes, so a single number can verify a whole catalog. The Star Chart publishes roots of this kind.

Dual ledger. Two records of the same event kept separately, so that one can be used to check the other. If the two disagree, you have found a problem, which is the entire purpose.

Star Chart. The public sky of seals: a live record of what has been anchored, with a hash pulse. It is a reference surface, not a canon. See the Star Chart room.

Immutable lattice. The house phrase for the anchored side of the system: records that are append-only, so history cannot be quietly rewritten. When a page says "immutable lattice" it means "there is a receipt and it does not move".

Witness. A page that watches public feeds — weather, seismic, orbital — and shows them without claiming they are canon. Reference, not revelation. See the witness room.

God's Eye. The map view of the witness feeds: one globe instead of many tabs. Named with a deliberate amount of irony; the point is that a globe is not an authority.

SKYNET. The sentinel kernel: a local monitor that reports rather than acts. If the name makes you nervous, that is fair; the system is a watchdog process with a log, not a plan for the world.

ISRC. The international code that identifies a recorded track. If a recording has an ISRC it has an identity that survives a distributor change. The music page explains why that matters.

Canon vs reference. The house distinction. Canon is what I wrote and stand behind — the books, the essays, the terms. Reference is what the world reports — feeds, charts, third-party pages. A reference can be wrong. A canon can be edited, but only on the record.

How to use a glossary

Look up the word that stopped you, then close the tab. A glossary is a tool for re-entering a text, not a substitute for reading it. If a term is missing, tell me; the list is maintained, not frozen.
